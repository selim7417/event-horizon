How it works

Following light through curved spacetime

A camera normally gathers light that travels in straight lines. Near a black hole light falls, so this renderer runs the photon's journey in reverse: for each pixel it launches a ray from your eye and follows it back through the Schwarzschild geometry of a non-rotating black hole until it lands on the glowing disk, escapes to the stars, or crosses the event horizon.

The orbit of a photon

In Schwarzschild coordinates, with u = 1/r, a light ray obeys the Binet equation

d²u/dφ² + u = 3⁄2 rs u²

which is exactly the orbit of a particle pushed by the fictitious force a = −(3/2) h² x / r⁵, where h = |x × v| stays constant. The fragment shader integrates this with velocity Verlet, with each step bounded by the integrator's local error: up to 480 steps for every pixel, every frame. The camera's local frame is converted to coordinates exactly, so the shadow edge lands within half a percent of the analytic critical impact parameter, 3√3⁄2 rs.

The colours are physics

The disk follows the Novikov–Thorne temperature profile, T ∝ r−3/4(1 − √(rin/r))1/4, with its inner edge at the innermost stable circular orbit, 3 rs. Colours come from integrating Planck's law against the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions. Because Iν/ν³ is conserved along a ray, a blackbody at temperature T seen with frequency shift g is exactly a blackbody at g·T, so the Doppler shift, the beaming and the gravitational redshift all come from one lookup:

g = √(1 − rs/r) · √(1 − v²) ⁄ (1 − Ω bz)

Here v is the orbital speed of the gas, Ω its angular velocity and bz the photon's angular momentum about the disk axis. The same shift is applied to every star.

Three ways to be near a black hole

What you see depends on how you move. A hovering observer fights gravity with rockets and sees the universe shrink into a blueshifted window overhead. An orbiting observer sees the sky aberrated by their orbital speed. A falling observer moves with the light and sees a calmer sky. Each view applies relativistic aberration and Doppler shift for the observer's velocity before tracing begins.

Built from scratch

  • WebGL2 with hand-written GLSL. No engine and no libraries.
  • A procedural Milky Way, nebulae and a distant galaxy baked once into an HDR cubemap; stars drawn analytically, widened and dimmed by how much lensing compresses each pixel.
  • HDR pipeline: physically based bloom, auto exposure, AgX tone mapping, film grain.
  • Temporal anti-aliasing that blends jittered frames while the view is still, and a photo mode that saves a supersampled still.
  • Step sizes bounded by the integrator's own error, and dynamic resolution driven by GPU timer queries, so the frame rate holds on anything from a phone to a desktop GPU.
  • The light-path diagram re-integrates the same equations on the CPU.
  • A Web Audio drone whose pitch follows the blueshift at your position.

What it leaves out

The black hole does not spin, so there is no frame dragging. The disk is geometrically thin, and its turbulence is a texture rather than a fluid simulation. Light travel time across the scene is ignored.
